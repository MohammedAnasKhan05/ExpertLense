# Expert 4 — Italy

**Expert name:** Dr. Marco Rossi
**Role:** Director of Urology, Regional Teaching Hospital
**Country:** Italy
**Interview Topic:** European Robotic Surgery Market
**Transcript Type:** Synthetic test transcript for evaluation

**[00:00] Interviewer:** Could you describe the current adoption of robotic surgery in Italian hospitals?

**[00:18] Dr. Marco Rossi:** Adoption is increasing, but it is still concentrated in larger teaching hospitals and private centres. Regional hospitals are generally more cautious because they have tighter capital budgets and may not have enough procedures to justify the investment.

**[00:42] Interviewer:** What are the biggest barriers to adoption?

**[00:55] Dr. Marco Rossi:** The first barrier is definitely the initial investment. Hospitals also have to consider maintenance contracts, disposable instruments, staff training, and whether they can generate enough procedure volume. Availability of trained surgeons is another important issue.

**[01:22] Interviewer:** How important is the economic case and ROI when hospitals purchase a robotic system?

**[01:35] Dr. Marco Rossi:** It is important, but I would not describe it as the only deciding factor. Hospital management looks at the total cost of ownership and expected utilization. If a hospital can only perform a small number of procedures, the economics become difficult. However, clinical strategy and the ability to attract surgeons can also influence the decision.

**[02:05] Interviewer:** So would you say a positive ROI guarantees that a hospital will purchase a system?

**[02:17] Dr. Marco Rossi:** No, absolutely not. A strong economic case helps, but it does not guarantee approval. Hospitals may still postpone a purchase because of competing capital projects, staffing limitations, or a lack of trained surgeons.

**[02:35] Interviewer:** How important is surgeon training?

**[02:47] Dr. Marco Rossi:** Training is critical. You need more than one surgeon who can use the platform if you want consistent utilization. Training also takes time, and that can become a bottleneck when hospitals are trying to expand robotic surgery.

**[03:12] Interviewer:** What about clinical outcomes?

**[03:25] Dr. Marco Rossi:** Clinical outcomes are important because hospitals need to demonstrate that the technology provides value for patients. But good clinical outcomes alone do not automatically justify the purchase. They have to be considered alongside utilization, costs, staffing, and the hospital's broader strategy.

**[03:50] Interviewer:** How do you expect adoption to change over the next three to five years?

**[04:02] Dr. Marco Rossi:** I expect continued growth, particularly in high-volume centres. I would be cautious about applying the growth rate of individual hospitals to the entire Italian market. Some centres may increase their robotic procedures by more than 15 percent annually, but that does not mean the whole market will grow at the same rate.

**[04:29] Interviewer:** What could accelerate adoption?

**[04:38] Dr. Marco Rossi:** Lower equipment costs would help. More training capacity would also make a difference. If hospitals can increase utilization and reduce the cost per procedure, the business case becomes easier to justify.

**[04:55] Interviewer:** What could slow adoption?

**[05:03] Dr. Marco Rossi:** Capital budget constraints are probably the biggest issue. A hospital may support robotic surgery clinically but still delay the purchase because it has other infrastructure investments that are considered more urgent.

**[05:20] Interviewer:** What is a typical purchasing timeline?

**[05:32] Dr. Marco Rossi:** I would say roughly 6 to 12 months when funding has already been approved. If the purchase needs to go through a new capital budgeting cycle, it can take considerably longer. There isn't one fixed timeline for every hospital.

**[05:55] Interviewer:** Who is normally involved in the decision?

**[06:04] Dr. Marco Rossi:** It is usually not a single person. Surgeons provide the clinical justification, procurement evaluates suppliers and contracts, finance looks at the economics, and hospital leadership makes the final capital decision.

**[06:22] Interviewer:** If you had to identify the single most important factor in adoption, what would it be?

**[06:30] Dr. Marco Rossi:** I would not choose one factor. Adoption depends on the combination of clinical need, procedure volume, trained staff, capital availability, and the expected economic value. Focusing on only one of these can give a misleading picture.

**[06:48] Interviewer:** Is there anything else that is important when comparing robotic surgery markets across Europe?

**[06:55] Dr. Marco Rossi:** Yes. I would avoid assuming that the purchasing behaviour is identical across countries. Funding structures, procurement processes, hospital size, and training capacity can differ significantly. Comparisons should therefore distinguish between country-level evidence and individual hospital examples.

**[07:12] Interviewer:** Thank you.

**[07:15] Dr. Marco Rossi:** Thank you.
